Abstract
We analyse single-trial standard-versus-target classification in the Nencki–Symfonia visual oddball
task using 39 task-compliant participants, 21,878 correct trials (13.49% targets) and preprocessed 128-
channel epochs at 250 Hz. The equal-participant-weight parietal target–standard effect is 5.05±2.28 µV
in 300–500 ms.
Under five-fold participant-wise evaluation, EEGNet achieves balanced accuracy
0.903 ± 0.021, ROC-AUC 0.970 ± 0.013 and average precision 0.865 ± 0.045. A matched 128-channel
shrinkage-LDA control reaches ROC-AUC 0.949 ± 0.022, showing that electrode count alone does not
explain the network’s advantage. Five further EEGNet models discriminate correct distractors from
standards, with neither class involving a button press, reaching ROC-AUC 0.926 ± 0.015. Saliency
is computed only on positive test inputs in each outer fold. The pooled target profile peaks at 504 ms,
places 40.7% mass in 300–600 ms and correlates with the OOF ERP contrast at r = 0.253. These
controls establish discrimination without a motor-response contrast and explain part of the classifier’s
temporal and spatial sensitivity. Specific motor contributions to target decoding remain unresolved
because stimulus identity and relevance also differ.

1
Introduction
1.1
Background and motivation
Electroencephalography (EEG) captures the timing of stimulus responses.
Averaging stimulus-locked
epochs reveals event-related potentials (ERPs), whereas target detection requires predictions from individ-
ual, noisy trials. An average ERP and a single-trial classifier may emphasise different information.
In the visual oddball task, frequent standards are interspersed with rare targets and distractors. Only
targets require a button press. The primary classification is standard (0) versus target (1); distractors sup-
port physiological comparisons and a no-button classification control. We analyse ERPs, engineer features,
compare conventional models with EEGNet and examine network sensitivity.
1.2
Research questions and scope
The project addresses the three research questions set for the mini project:
1. RQ1: What is the best single trial classification result for frequent versus target stimuli?
2. RQ2: Is Deep Learning better for single trial classification compared to standard machine learning tech-
niques?
3. RQ3: Are the features that the deep learning model discovers explainable?
The study combines group physiology, individual ERP checks and offline cross-participant prediction. The
three questions are answered directly in Section 5.
2
Related work
2.1
P300 physiology and ERP classification
Polich’s integrative account distinguishes a relatively fronto-central P3a associated with attentional orienting
from a more parietal P3b associated with task-relevant processing and context updating [1]. This motivates
spatial and temporal ERP comparisons, but does not prescribe the numerical thresholds used in this project.
Latency variation and overlapping responses complicate a fixed-window interpretation.
Regularised classifiers are appropriate references for single-trial ERP decoding. Blankertz et al. dis-
cuss shrinkage LDA for this setting [2], and a review of BCI classifiers finds shrinkage LDA particularly
useful when training samples are small, while reporting that deep learning had not yet shown convincing
improvement over state-of-the-art BCI methods [3]. Laton et al. combine ERP descriptors in a different clas-
sification problem [4]; our implementation adapts peak-related descriptors to individual trials, rather than
claiming to reproduce their clinical study. The Nencki–Symfonia publication supplies the dataset context
and behavioural task definition [5].
2.2
Compact neural networks and interpretability
EEGNet uses temporal and depthwise spatial convolutions to learn a compact representation from multi-
channel EEG [6]. It provides a suitable test of end-to-end signal learning against fixed summaries. How-
ever, model sensitivity and signal amplitude are different quantities. Haufe et al. warn that backward-model
parameters need not identify the origin of a discriminative process [7]. We apply the same caution when
relating input gradients to scalp ERPs.

3
Methodology
3.1
Dataset, labels and analysis population
The project uses the visual oddball task from GigaDB dataset 100990 [5]. It contains 42 participants recorded
in BrainVision format at 1000 Hz, with 127 acquired electrodes and online reference FCz. Re-adding FCz
yields 128 channels. The project assigns a Colin27 10–05 montage and maps O9/O10 to I1/I2 positions.
Channel order is harmonised across recordings.
A typical session includes 660 stimuli, approximately 75% standards, 12% targets and 12% distractors.
Markers S5, S6 and S7 indicate these conditions, and S1 indicates a button press. A target is correct when a
response occurs 100–1400 ms after stimulus onset; standards and distractors are correct when no response
occurs. Some recordings have irregular lengths or multiple segments, which the pipeline handles explicitly.
Participants sub-02, sub-14 and sub-17 are excluded from the primary analyses because they misun-
derstood the task and frequently responded to distractors. This is a predeclared behavioural exclusion in the
project configuration. Their epochs remain available for QC and diagnostic ERP comparisons. No additional
participant is excluded according to an ERP threshold or classifier performance. Classification uses correct
retained standard and target trials only, defining performance conditional on successful task behaviour.
3.2
Preprocessing pipeline
The preprocessing notebook implements the following steps using MNE-Python [8] (Figure 1):
1. Task restriction and channel preparation. Crop non-task recording periods, re-add FCz, harmonise
channel order and assign the montage. Mark segment joins as bad boundaries.
2. Bad-channel detection. On a 200 Hz, 1–40 Hz, median-referenced copy, flag a channel if the robust
z-score of log-IQR has magnitude > 4, median correlation with six neighbours is < 0.4, or neighbour
correlation is < −0.8. Interpolate bad channels with spherical splines and apply an average reference.
3. ICA estimation. Fit 40 extended-Picard components [9] on a 1–100 Hz copy at 250 Hz, with seed
97. ICLabel identifies candidate ocular, cardiac and muscle components [10, 11]; components in those
classes are removed at probability ≥0.8. One additional blink component is manually excluded in each
of sub-13, sub-14 and sub-36. These decisions apply to the corresponding fitted ICA solutions.
4. Analysis filtering and cleaning. Filter the original-rate recording at 0.5–40 Hz with a zero-phase FIR
filter and apply the ICA solution. The low-pass precedes decimation.
5. Epoching and rejection. Extract −200 to 1000 ms epochs, apply the −200 to 0 ms baseline, and deci-
mate by four to 250 Hz. Reject epochs overlapping bad boundaries or exceeding 150 µV peak-to-peak on
any channel. Store float32 arrays with 128 channels and 301 samples, together with behavioural metadata.
“Raw epochs” in the deep-learning implementation refers to these preprocessed samples before feature en-
gineering. It does not mean unfiltered acquisition data.
3.3
Quality-control interpretation
An automated epoch audit (src/preprocessing/audit_epochs.py) checks channel order, montage, time
axis, filter/baseline metadata, finite values, labels, reference, rejection bounds and agreement with QC
counts. It also compares feature rows, labels, reaction times and recomputed Pz late-window means with
the feature table. Baseline correction precedes decimation, so the mean of retained 250 Hz baseline samples
need not be exactly zero. These residuals are recorded rather than corrected retrospectively.
3.4

ERPs are computed from correct retained trials, first within each participant and then with equal weights
across the 39 included participants. Thus participants with more retained trials do not dominate the group

3.5

The feature extractor uses 12 channels: Fz, FCz, Cz, CPz, Pz, Oz, F3/F4, C3/C4 and P3/P4. Amplitude

descriptive; a fixed interval does not itself isolate P3a or P3b. The 300–600 ms feature window is distinct
from the primary ERP verification window.

3.7
EEGNet architecture
The PyTorch implementation follows EEGNet-8,2 [6]. Each input is a preprocessed epoch with shape B ×
1×128×301. The first temporal convolution learns eight filters of length 125 samples (500 ms). A depthwise
spatial convolution spans all channels, with depth multiplier two, producing 16 feature maps. The second
block combines depthwise temporal and pointwise convolutions. The final linear layer outputs two logits.

3.8
Optimisation and validation
Training uses Adam with learning rate 10−3, batch size 64, class-weighted cross-entropy and at most 50
epochs. Inputs are divided by a global standard deviation estimated from the optimisation subset. Early
stopping uses validation loss with patience 15. For each outer fold, a stratified random 15% of training trials
is reserved for validation.
The internal split is by trial, not by participant. Optimisation and validation can contain the same partic-
ipants, although the outer test remains disjoint. Consequently, the experiment evaluates unseen outer-test

4
Results
4.1
Data quality and cohort retention
The epoch audit passed its checks for all 42 files (27,034 retained epochs) and the 21,878-row classification
feature table. Features contained no nonfinite numerical values. The QC summary reports 2.9±3.4 interpo-
lated channels and 15.0 ± 4.7 removed ICA components per participant. The average rejected fraction was
3.0 ± 7.8%, with a range of 0–49.5%; thus good aggregate retention does not imply uniform data quality.
After the behavioural exclusion, classification includes 18,927 standards and 2,951 targets from 39 par-
ticipants. Maximum absolute baseline means per participant range from 0.193 to 1.006 µV, consistent with
correction before decimation. ICA decisions were reviewed separately: for the three manually excluded
blink components, ICLabel was rerun from the raw recordings and reproduced the reported probabilities
(0.78, 0.70 and 0.59).
4.2
Group ERPs and operational checks
Mean correct-trial Pz amplitude in 300–500 ms was 6.97 ± 2.74 µV for targets and 1.92 ± 1.49 µV for
standards. The paired difference was 5.05 ± 2.28 µV, t(38) = 13.82, p = 2.14 × 10−16 and dz = 2.21. SD
describes variability across participants.

4.3
Scalp distribution and feature discrimination
The mean 300–500 ms scalp maps show a parietal positive target-minus-standard effect (Figure 3). Dis-
tractors show a relative frontal tendency compared with targets in 26/39 participants. The corresponding
contrast, (DFz −DPz)−(TFz −TPz), averages 1.43±2.46 µV. This describes a relative distribution, rather
than absolute frontal positivity.
The distractor-minus-standard mean in 300–500 ms is −1.06 ± 1.94 µV at Fz, 0.71 ± 1.54 µV at Cz and
3.54 ± 1.67 µV at Pz (SD across 39 participants). Thus the measured distractor response is predominantly
parietal in this window; it is not a canonical frontal P3a. This pattern is consistent with Polich’s integra-
tive account: a frontally distributed P3a to rare non-target stimuli is most evident when the target–standard
discrimination is diﬀicult, whereas easily discriminated distractors elicit a P3 with a parietal maximum re-
sembling the target P3b [1]. Targets and standards are easy to discriminate in this task: the task-compliant
cohort missed 5.6% of targets and responded to only 0.27% of standards. Both conditions have no but-
ton press on the analysed correct trials. This descriptive ERP result complements the no-button classifier
analysis below. Figure 4 shows both difference waves at Fz, Cz and Pz.

4.6
Channel-matched LDA control
Shrinkage LDA receives either the original 12 channels or all 128. Each channel is represented by twelve
fixed 100 ms mean-amplitude bins spanning −200 to 1000 ms; bins are left-closed and right-open, except the
final bin includes 1000 ms. The resulting 144- and 1,536-feature representations use training-fold standard-
isation, empirical LDA priors, threshold 0.5 and the same five outer test participant groups. No bin-width
or hyperparameter search was performed.
Adding electrodes improves binned LDA ROC-AUC by 0.012. EEGNet still leads the 128-channel con-
trol by 0.021 ROC-AUC, 0.043 AP and 7.3 balanced-accuracy percentage points. Electrode count alone
therefore does not explain the lead. Temporal representation and optimisation differ, so the conclusion con-
cerns the tested pipelines rather than an isolated architectural cause.

5
Discussion
RQ1: EEGNet achieves ROC-AUC 0.970, balanced accuracy 90.3% and AP 0.865 on unseen outer-test
participants. RQ2: Yes, it leads the tested ML pipelines, including 128-channel LDA (ROC-AUC 0.949).
RQ3: Partly: all-five-fold test saliency provides temporal and partial spatial correspondence, while no-
button distractor decoding reaches ROC-AUC 0.926.
5.1
RQ1: best single-trial performance
The final EEGNet result is balanced accuracy 0.903 ± 0.021, ROC-AUC 0.970 ± 0.013 and AP 0.865 ±
0.045. The outer participant-wise split tests people absent from the corresponding classifier’s training and
validation. Performance is conditional on correct behaviour from the task-compliant cohort. Preprocessing
is fitted separately per recording; external-data generalisation and online BCI latency or information-transfer
rate have not been evaluated.
5.2
RQ2: whether deep learning improves performance
Yes, for the tested pipelines. EEGNet exceeds logistic regression by 2.82 balanced-accuracy percentage
points and the 128-channel LDA control by 0.021 ROC-AUC. The matched-channel control shows that
electrode count alone does not explain the advantage. Because balanced accuracy depends on each model’s

ms bins while EEGNet learns temporal and spatial filters from individual samples, leaving representation
and optimisation as additional differences. The gain is modest because the P300 is a low-dimensional, largely
linear spatio-temporal pattern: a shrinkage LDA that averages over channels and time already captures most
of the separable information [2], so additional model capacity mainly refines spatial and temporal filtering
rather than revealing new discriminative structure. This agrees with the conclusion of Lotte et al. on deep
learning for BCIs [3]. Participant-level paired comparisons support within-cohort consistency, subject to
their dependence and exploratory nature.
5.3
RQ3: interpretability in relation to the P300
EEGNet is partly interpretable. Test-only saliency from all five folds relates target sensitivity to the late
response: a 504 ms temporal peak, 40.7% mass in 300–600 ms and pooled spatial r = 0.253 (0.203 within
300–600 ms). The spatial match is incomplete and varies between folds. Notably, target saliency concen-
trates over left-central sites (C1, FCC3h, C3) near the mean reaction time, whereas this cluster disappears
in the no-button distractor task in favour of anterior sites (AFz, AFF2h, FC1), where residual ocular activity
is a plausible contributor. That contrast is consistent with motor-related sensitivity in the target classifier
alongside genuine stimulus decoding, while input gradients measure local predictive sensitivity—which can
include noise suppression—rather than neural activation amplitude [7].
The distractor model reaches ROC-AUC 0.926 without button presses. This establishes stimulus dis-
crimination without a motor-response contrast. It strengthens the stimulus-related evidence but does not
quantify how much the target model uses motor information, because the tasks differ in stimulus identity
and relevance as well as response requirements. Response-locked analyses and feature interventions would
test that mechanism more directly.
5.4
Limitations and threats to validity
1. Response and stimulus differences. The no-button control is complete, but it does not isolate motor
information in target decoding; stimulus and task-relevance differences remain.
2. Internal validation. EEGNet validation is trial-stratified and SVM probability estimation is not ex-
plicitly grouped by participant. Outer test participants remain disjoint, while participant-grouped inner
selection would better match the intended generalisation.
3. Exploratory interpretation and training budget. Gradient maps are sensitivity explanations. Feature
rankings use the whole cohort, no repeated-seed or nested tuning study was performed, four of five target
folds hit the 50-epoch cap (fold 3 peaked at epoch 48), and paired p-values are unadjusted and dependent
across fitted models.
4. Scope. All analyses use a single run of the preprocessing pipeline; alternative cleaning settings, such
as other ICLabel thresholds, were not compared. No independent dataset or online BCI evaluation is
available.
6
Conclusion
The visual oddball task shows a parietal target–standard effect of 5.05 µV in 300–500 ms. EEGNet achieves
ROC-AUC 0.970 and balanced accuracy 90.3%, ahead of the tested feature baselines and 128-channel LDA
(ROC-AUC 0.949). This answers RQ1 and supports RQ2 for the implemented pipelines.
For RQ3, saliency is evaluated on every outer test fold, and a completed no-button distractor control
reaches ROC-AUC 0.926. Together these analyses support stimulus-related discrimination with partial
physiological correspondence. They leave target-specific motor contributions and causal neural sources
unresolved. The remaining priorities are response-locked analyses, participant-grouped inner validation,
feature interventions and external replication.
